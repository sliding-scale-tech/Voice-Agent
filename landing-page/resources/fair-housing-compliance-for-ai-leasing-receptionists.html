Fair Housing Compliance for AI Leasing Receptionists

AI tools are showing up in more leasing offices every year, and property managers are right to ask whether that creates legal exposure. It's not a hypothetical concern. In 2024, a federal court approved a $2.275 million settlement against SafeRent Solutions, a tenant screening company, after its algorithm was found to violate the Fair Housing Act. If you're evaluating any AI tool for your leasing process, here's what actually matters, and how Leaseline is built to avoid the mistakes that caused that case.

What Fair Housing law actually protects

The Fair Housing Act protects seven classes: race, color, religion, sex, national origin, familial status, and disability. A policy doesn't have to be intentionally discriminatory to violate the Act. If a neutral-looking process produces unequal outcomes for a protected class, that's called disparate impact, and it's enough to create liability on its own. HUD has been explicit that this applies to AI and algorithmic tools the same way it applies to a human leasing agent's decisions.

What went wrong with SafeRent

SafeRent's tool scored rental applicants using credit history and general debt, then recommended whether to approve or deny them. The algorithm disproportionately scored Black and Hispanic applicants lower, and it penalized applicants using housing vouchers without accounting for the fact that public housing authorities pay most of a voucher holder's rent directly. The court found this produced a discriminatory effect, regardless of whether anyone intended it to. As part of the settlement, SafeRent was barred from using AI-generated scores for voucher applicants at all.

The core problem wasn't that SafeRent used AI. It's that SafeRent's AI made a judgment call about people, using data that happened to correlate with race and income.

How Leaseline is built differently

Leaseline never scores, ranks, approves, or denies an applicant. It does one job: collect five fields (unit type, move-in date, budget, pets, contact info) and book a tour. Every actual leasing decision stays with your team.

The AI is not designed to ask about anything outside those five fields. It has no script, no path, and no reason to ask about race, familial status, disability, national origin, or any other protected characteristic, because none of those questions exist in what it's permitted to say. This isn't a policy Leaseline promises to follow. It's a hard limit on what the AI can do.

The order of the questions can shift depending on what a caller mentions first, and the AI won't re-ask something already covered. But the outcome doesn't depend on that. Whether someone qualifies is decided by a separate rule-based check applied to the same five fields every time, not a judgment call made mid-conversation. How someone sounds, including their accent, never factors into it. That consistency is a direct defense against the kind of uneven treatment that creates fair housing exposure in the first place.

Anything outside those two topics, leasing inquiries and existing-tenant issues, gets escalated to a human rather than answered on the spot. The AI doesn't improvise when it's unsure. It hands off.

What Leaseline doesn't do

Leaseline doesn't check credit. It doesn't check criminal history. It doesn't check eviction history. Those three are the highest-risk areas for disparate impact in AI screening tools, and Leaseline doesn't touch any of them, because it isn't a screening tool. It's a receptionist.
